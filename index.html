Founders' Role Charter

A technology company needs about two dozen jobs done. It does not need two dozen people. What it needs is for every one of those jobs to have a name attached to it — so that nothing important is quietly nobody's. Work down the catalog and claim each one out loud.

      Before the list: split by domain, not by task

      The failure mode for a two-person company isn't taking on too much work. It's dividing the work task by task. Tasks get traded back and forth until neither of you can say who is holding a thing, and the answer to "why didn't that happen?" is always "I thought you had it."

      Domains don't do that. A domain has one owner, permanently, and the owner is accountable for the outcome whether or not they personally did the work. That's the whole trick.

      The natural seam in a technology company runs down the middle: the work of making the thing, and the work of running the business that sells it. The seam can sit somewhere else if your strengths say so — but it has to sit somewhere, and you both have to be able to say out loud where it is.

      Inside

      Build & Run the Product

      What gets made, how it's made, whether it stays up, and whether it's actually done. Deep work, long blocks, few interruptions. This side of the seam is protected time or it doesn't happen.

      Outside

      Build & Run the Business

      Who buys, what they pay, whether they stay, and where the money goes. Interrupt-driven, relationship-heavy, calendar-shaped. This side of the seam absorbs the chaos so the other side doesn't have to.

      Own nowNeeds a named owner today. If it's nobody's, it's already hurting you.
      ShareOne of you leads it, both of you touch it. Still name the lead.
      LaterA real job, but not yet. Fold it into an Own now role and revisit.
      BuyHire a firm or a service. Doing this yourself costs the wrong hours.

      Six rules for splitting a company between two people

      Split by domain, not by task

      Tasks get traded until neither of you knows who's holding one. Domains have an owner and stay owned — even on the weeks the owner didn't personally do the work.

      Don't split the customer

      One voice owns each relationship. A customer should never have to guess which of you to email. The other founder can be in the conversation; only one is responsible for it.

      Money is visible to both, owned by one

      Both of you see every account, always. One of you is accountable for the forecast being right. Shared visibility and shared ownership are different things, and only the first one is healthy.

      Name the tiebreaker before you need one

      Not "we'll talk it through" — a named person, per domain, whose call ends the discussion. Two equal owners with no tiebreaker is the single most common way a small company stalls out.

      Every role gets a backup, on paper

      For each role, write down who covers it for two weeks if the owner is out sick. Where the honest answer is "nobody," you've just found your first hire.

      Business hours are a container, not a mood

      Write down when business conversations happen — and when they don't. When two people run a company and share a house, the real risk isn't the workload. It's the meeting that never ends.

      Write each role down in six lines

      A role that only lives in a conversation isn't a role. Use the same six fields for every one of them — including the ones you'll hire for later, which is how you end up with a job posting already written.

      Role
      The title, as an outsider would recognize it
      Owns
      The outcome, in one sentence — not a list of tasks
      Alone
      Decisions this person makes without checking
      Together
      Decisions that require both founders
      Measured
      The one number that says whether it's going well
      Backup
      Who covers it for two weeks

      The first five hires, in order

      This sequence is genuinely ordered — each hire depends on the one before it existing.

      1. Bookkeeper — fractional

        Buys back the most hours per dollar of anything on this list, and makes every financial decision after it possible.

      2. Support & Implementation

        Your first W-2. It's the highest-volume, most interruptible work in the company, and it is the specific thing stopping you from building.

      3. Second engineer

        Hire when the roadmap is blocked on hands, not on knowing what to build. Hiring before that just makes the confusion faster.

      4. Salesperson

        Only after you've closed enough deals yourselves to write down how it's done. Never hire someone to figure out sales for you — that's a founder's job to solve first.

      5. Operations Manager

        Hire when the written procedures already exist and someone simply needs to run them. Not to invent them.

      How to actually use this

      Go down the catalog together and claim domains out loud, one at a time. Say the words "I own that" — not "I guess I've been doing that."

      When you're through, look at what neither of you claimed. Everything left is one of two things: your next hire, or something you've decided to do badly on purpose. Both are acceptable answers. Not knowing which is not.

      A fourteen-day program · four modules

      Stepping Into the Roles

      Every role you claimed becomes a two-week module program. Four modules, each with the input you have to gather, the milestone that says you're through it, and the one thing you need from the other founder. Check things off as you go — it saves.
